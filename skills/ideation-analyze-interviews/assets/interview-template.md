# Ideation interview template
A reusable conversation guide based on The Mom Test by Rob Fitzpatrick.

## T2. Open naturally
Suggested opening: “I’m learning how [people in their role/context] handle [broad area]. I’m trying to understand [specific gap], and your experience with [true, specific reason] would help. Could you walk me through a recent example?”
For a scheduled meeting, briefly explain your broad purpose, current stage, what you do not understand, why this person can help, and your request. Say you have nothing to sell only when that is true. Then move to the first question.
For a casual encounter, start with the relevant question directly. Do not force a meeting onto a conversation that is already happening.
Notes: “Is it okay if I jot down a few notes?” If recording, get permission and explain the actual intended use and audience. Do not promise confidentiality or limited sharing you cannot honor.

## T3. Start broad enough to establish importance
“What has been taking most of your attention in [area] recently?”
“Which things are you trying to improve or resolve right now?”
“What happened recently that made those priorities important?”
If our suspected problem is not raised, explore whether it matters without insisting that it does. Record whether the topic arose spontaneously or only after prompting. Zoom in immediately only when prior evidence establishes its importance for this segment.
Priority evidence / competing priorities: [ ]
If they do not care, learn why and finish politely. Do not pitch harder.

## T4. Reconstruct a specific past event
“Tell me about the last time [event] happened.”
“What triggered it? What happened first, and then what?”
“Who was involved? Which tools, documents, or handoffs did you use?”
“Could you show me how you do that, or a recent example?” Ask only for material they can share.
“What went smoothly, and what, if anything, was difficult? What did you do next?”
“What were you trying to achieve? Why did that matter?”

## T5. Establish consequences and existing behavior
“What effect, if any, did that have?”
If a consequence is reported: “What did that involve in time, money, missed work, or another consequence?”
“How did you estimate that?”
“Has this happened on other occasions? Tell me about a recent example, if so.”
“How do you deal with it now? What works well about that?”
“What have you tried before? How did you find those options?”
“What happened when you tried them? Why did you keep or stop using them?”
“What have you spent on this already — including people or outside services?”
If there has been no attempt to solve it: “How did you decide whether to address it?” Distinguish low priority from a real obstacle; do not assume either.

## T6. Optional B2B and multiple-stakeholder questions
“Walk me through the last purchase or change of this kind.”
“Whose budget paid for it? Who initiated, approved, used, or could block it?”
“What did approval involve? What requirements or policies mattered?”
“What affected the timing? How long did it actually take?”
“Who else has a different view of this workflow?”
Budget source / owner / approval steps / procurement / constraints: [ ]
Missing perspectives to investigate separately: [ ]
For consumer or family purchases, adapt this to who pays, chooses, and uses. For marketplaces and partner-dependent products, learn each side’s goals and constraints separately.

## T7. Recover when answers get vague
Compliment → acknowledge briefly, then return to their current behavior. Do not count praise as demand.
“Usually / always / never” → ask for the latest concrete example or an exception.
“Would / might / definitely will buy” → treat as an untested intention; return to past behavior, or seek a real commitment if in solution mode.
Feature request → ask what it enables, why it matters, how they manage without it, and whether it has blocked an actual purchase. Capture the need before deciding on a feature.
Strong emotion → ask what happened and why it mattered. Emotion is a cue to investigate, not proof of willingness to pay.
Accidental pitch → pause, acknowledge that you got ahead of yourself, and return to their story. Let them finish even when their understanding is imperfect.
Question repair examples:
Instead of “Would you use an app for this?” ask “How did you handle the last instance?”
Instead of “How much would you pay?” ask “What are you spending on this now, and from which budget?”
Instead of “Do you like this idea?” ask about the present workflow and consequences.

## T8. Optional solution and commitment branch
Use only after sufficient discovery, when showing a real offer or prototype can answer the next uncertainty. Discovery can succeed with concrete facts and no purchase request.
Offer shown, terms, and when it was introduced: [ ]

Appropriate next step: [specific trial with scope and date / introduction to a named decision maker / review with the relevant team / paid pilot or deposit where appropriate]
“What would need to happen to take that next step?”
Then make a concrete request: “Would you agree to [specific action] by [date], with [scope and terms]?” Record the answer separately from completion. This asks for a present decision about a real next step, not a hypothetical opinion about a future product.
Ask clearly; allow a genuine no. Do not manufacture scarcity or pressure someone into agreement.

If yes, move to the next uncertainty or a practical test. Do not use a fixed interview count as proof of demand. If results remain inconsistent, inspect segmentation and question quality first.

## T11. Five-minute pocket version
Know the one current learning goal. Ask about a recent relevant event. Explore what they did, why it mattered, and how they deal with it. Follow the strongest signal; stop when you have learned what you came for. Ask for a useful referral if appropriate. Record the evidence immediately afterward. This is a short route through T1–T10, not a separate validation method.
