Test whether you can deliver and measure incremental Tuesday customers through one small, manual promotion. The five conditional statements do not establish demand for an app or your ability to bring 50 additional people. Another 20 broad interviews would leave the delivery uncertainty largely untouched.

Run a one-week feasibility experiment with one bar, one offer, and one local distribution channel. Do not build an app. This is a proposed experiment only; nothing should be launched or promised yet.

**Days 1–2: Define the offer and measurement.** Choose one of the five owners willing to provide recent comparable Tuesday attendance and transaction figures. Clarify their current promotion spending, who can approve an experiment, venue capacity, and what counts as a valuable additional customer. Have the authorized owner agree to a small test and a modest promotion the bar can actually fulfill. Record that agreement; do not interpret enthusiasm as acceptance.

Use a simple reservation or offer page and a unique redemption code. Select one geographically targeted channel that can run this week, such as a small paid local promotion. Decide on the channel after checking its availability and cost. Ask the venue to record redemptions and total Tuesday attendance consistently.

A code redemption shows attribution to the promotion, not necessarily an incremental customer. Regulars might have visited anyway. Compare attendance with recent similar Tuesdays and ask redeemers whether they had already planned to come, while acknowledging that a single-night comparison and self-report cannot establish causality.

**Days 3–5: Test distribution.** Run the agreed promotion within the cap, tracking spend, reach where available, visits to the offer, and reservations. If no appropriate Tuesday falls inside the seven-day window, start the experiment in a window that contains one; do not replace attendance evidence with clicks. Avoid spending the full budget on a channel that cannot reach the venue’s local audience.

**Tuesday and the following day: Observe and decide.** Count actual visits, redemptions, purchases where the owner can share aggregate figures, and no-shows. Review results with the decision maker. If the result warrants it, ask whether they will accept a clearly priced repeat test with defined terms and a date. Record acceptance separately from payment and do not count another conditional “yes” as a sale.

**Maximum budget**

| Item | Cap |
|---|---:|
| Local distribution | $140 |
| Simple page, tracking, or printed material | $20 |
| Agreed offer subsidy and contingency | $40 |
| Total | $200 |

Use free tools where possible. Any subsidy must have a redemption cap so the total cannot exceed $200. State whether the bar contributes a discount or staff time; those are costs even if they do not come out of your cash budget.

Before launch, use the owner’s contribution margin and acceptable acquisition cost to set a minimum useful result. Without those numbers, an attendance target would be arbitrary. Report cost per redeemed visit separately from estimated cost per incremental customer. The latter will be uncertain in this small test.

Continue only if you see actual attendance attributable to the channel, economics worth testing further, and a concrete owner commitment to a next step. Reservations without visits point to a conversion problem. Visits at an uneconomic cost point to a channel or offer problem. An owner who will only pay for 50 customers has not validated a smaller service.

This experiment can establish whether there is a plausible path to acquiring customers and obtaining a real buying commitment. It cannot prove that you can reliably deliver 50 extra customers, validate all five bars, or justify a large app build.
