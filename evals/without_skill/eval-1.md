Use the first interviews to understand recent reporting work and competing priorities. “Would you buy an AI dashboard?” invites encouragement, price questions without a buying context produce guesses, and feature wish lists do not tell you which problems matter.

Treat “sales organizations with 25–250 salespeople” as a starting recruitment range. Record company size, participant role, reporting responsibilities, and who approves purchases so you can compare similar participants. Interview people who prepare reports, people who use them, and purchasing decision makers separately where possible.

**Reusable 15-minute guide**

**0–1 minute: Set context**

“We’re learning how sales reporting works today. I’d like to hear about your actual process and recent examples. There’s no product to evaluate, and it’s useful to hear when things work well.”

**1–4 minutes: Reconstruct a recent report**

- “Tell me about the most recent sales report you prepared or used.”
- “What triggered it, who needed it, and what decision was it supposed to support?”
- “Walk me through what happened, from gathering information to using the report.”

If they do not own the process, ask who does rather than encouraging speculation.

**4–8 minutes: Establish consequences**

- “Where, if anywhere, did that report take more effort than expected?”
- “How much time did it take, and whose time was involved?”
- “What happened because of the delay, missing information, or extra work?”
- “When did this happen before? How often are these reports produced?”
- “Could you show me a redacted example or describe the steps in more detail?”

Do not assume that inconvenience creates material business consequences. Record their answer even if the process is adequate.

**8–11 minutes: Check existing investment and priority**

- “What have you tried to change about this process?”
- “What do you currently spend on tools or outside help for it?”
- “What happened the last time someone proposed an improvement?”
- “What other problems received attention or budget ahead of reporting?”

**11–14 minutes: Understand users and buying decisions**

- “Who creates the reports, who relies on them, and who owns improving the process?”
- “Tell me about the last similar tool your team purchased. Who requested it, evaluated it, and approved the money?”
- “What would an evaluation need to establish, based on that purchase?”

Ask users about their experience; do not treat their purchasing guesses as buyer evidence.

**14–15 minutes: Close with an appropriate next step**

- “What have I missed?”
- If relevant: “Would you be comfortable sharing a redacted report or introducing the person who owns this process?”

Record what is actually shared or arranged separately from what is merely offered.

**After each interview, capture:** participant role and organization context; recent reporting example; frequency; time and money involved; business consequences; existing tools and workarounds; attempted changes; competing priorities; buyer and approval process; exact quotes; unresolved questions; and completed next steps.

Your three original questions become:

| Original | More useful discovery question |
|---|---|
| Would you buy an AI dashboard? | What happened the last time reporting caused a problem, and what did you do about it? |
| How much would you pay? | What are you spending today, and how was the last comparable purchase approved? |
| Which features should we build? | Walk me through a specific task that is difficult today and the outcome you need from it. |

Look for repeated, specific problems in a coherent subgroup, meaningful consequences, and efforts or resources already committed to solving them. Compliments and feature requests are weak signals. Do not conclude demand or pricing from discovery alone; use what you learn to design a concrete offer and later test actual commitments. No customer evidence exists yet.
