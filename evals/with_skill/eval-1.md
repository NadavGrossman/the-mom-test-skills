# Discovery guide: AI reporting for sales teams

We have no customer evidence yet. The decision is whether reporting is important enough in a specific sales workflow to justify a practical solution test. These are proposed questions and hypotheses, not customer findings.

## T1. Research brief

The 25–250-salesperson segment is broad: a manager preparing a weekly review may have different needs from a sales operations analyst or executive consuming it. Provisionally start with the person who actually prepared the latest weekly sales review in a 25–75-person sales organization. This is an operational starting slice, not evidence that it is the best market. Include people whose process works well. A possible access route is existing sales-operations contacts or a relevant professional community; access is unconfirmed.

Record before each conversation:

- Date / interviewer / note-taker: [ ]
- Participant ID / role / organization: [ ]
- Source / relationship: [ ]
- Actual team size, reporting responsibility, cadence, and motivation: [ ]
- Decision role: [user / buyer / approver / blocker / unknown]
- Mode: discovery.
- Existing evidence: none for the idea; participant facts remain blank until supplied.
- Desk research: use authorized company information, if later available, to establish basic context. No external research or contact was undertaken here.

Missing perspectives include the report consumer, budget owner, purchasing approver, and any IT/security or data-access blocker. Their relevance must be established, not assumed. Interview the person doing the task rather than only the most senior person.

Customer risk: the process may already be good enough, or fixing it may lose to other priorities. Buyer risk: the user may lack budget and sponsorship. Delivery risks—data access, integration, accuracy, confidentiality, and reliable reporting—need practical tests once requirements are known. Interviews cannot prove that AI can deliver accurate reports or that buyers can be acquired economically.

## Three learning goals

| Goal and provisional belief | Neutral question | Evidence that changes the belief and decision |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Fatal risk: reporting may not be a meaningful priority. We have no evidence that it is. | “What has taken most of your attention in running the sales team recently? What happened that made that important?” | Reporting is absent, has minor consequences, or is explicitly satisfactory: do not build around presumed pain; investigate another problem or segment. |
| 2. Understand the job, consequences, and existing alternatives. Repeated manual work is only a hypothesis. | “Walk me through the last sales review, starting with how the information was prepared.” | A recent sequence, time estimates, consequences, and prior attempts identify a specific job worth testing—or show an adequate workaround. |
| 3. Identify who can fund and authorize a change. User and buyer may differ. | “Tell me about the last reporting tool or process change. Who initiated it, paid, approved, and used it?” | A real buying path informs whom to learn from next; no known sponsor or budget remains an unresolved risk, not a rejection. |

For a separate buyer conversation, adapt these same three goals to competing funded priorities, outcomes from the last reporting change, and actual approval/budget decisions. Do not substitute a user's guesses for the buyer's account.

## T2–T7. Flexible 15-minute guide

Use the main prompts; choose follow-ups based on what is learned. Let the participant finish. If reporting clearly is not important, learn why and close early.

**0–1 minute — opening.** “I'm exploring how sales teams prepare and use information to run the business. This is early research, and I don't yet know which parts matter most. Your role in [confirmed responsibility] would help me understand the process. Could we spend 15 minutes on a recent example? Is it okay if I jot down notes?”

**1–4 minutes — establish importance.** “What has taken most of your attention in running the sales team recently?” Follow with “What happened that made that a priority?” If reporting is not mentioned, ask once: “Where, if anywhere, does preparing or using reports fit among those priorities?” Mark spontaneous versus prompted mention.

**4–9 minutes — reconstruct an event.** “Walk me through the last sales review, starting with how the information was prepared.” Select probes:

- “Who did what, and which tools or documents did they use?”
- “What decisions was the report meant to support?”
- “What worked well? Where, if anywhere, did it get difficult?”
- “What effect, if any, did that have?” If a consequence is reported: “How did you estimate the time or cost?”
- “Could you show a recent example you are allowed to share?” Do not require confidential material.

**9–12 minutes — alternatives and priority.** “How do you handle that now?” Then choose: “What else have you tried? What happened?” “Has this happened on other occasions? Tell me about a recent example, if so.” “What have you already spent, including staff time or outside services?” If nothing has been tried: “How did you decide whether to address it?” Low priority and practical obstacles are different explanations.

**12–14 minutes — buying path, where relevant.** “Walk me through the last reporting tool or process change.” Probe budget source, initiator, approver, blockers, adoption effort, timing, and how alternatives were found. If no such purchase occurred, record that and ask about the closest comparable change without pretending the processes are identical.

**14–15 minutes — T9 close.** “What have I missed?” “Who else sees a different part of this process?” A referral is optional; clarify who and how only if one is offered. Thank them and confirm only agreed follow-ups.

**T7 recovery branches**

- Praise or “I'd buy it”: “Thanks. How did you handle the last instance?” Do not count the praise as demand.
- “Reporting is always awful”: “What happened the most recent time? Was there a recent time it worked well?”
- Feature request: “What would that let you accomplish? How do you handle it today? Has its absence blocked an actual purchase?”
- Strong emotion: “What happened that made it feel that way?” Do not infer urgency from emotion alone.
- Accidental pitch: “I got ahead of myself. Could we go back to what happened in your last review?”

## Question-quality audit

| Original | Replacement and reason |
|---|---|
| “Would you buy an AI dashboard?” | Reconstruct their last review and latest tool change. A hypothetical purchase does not establish behavior or priority. |
| “How much would you pay?” | Ask what they currently spend, from whose budget, and what happened in the last purchase. This describes existing behavior, not a validated price for a new product. |
| “Which features should we build?” | Ask what they were trying to accomplish, how they manage now, and where alternatives fell short. The team must design the solution. |

T8 is excluded from this first discovery guide: there is no defined offer to evaluate. A later concrete prototype review or pilot may be useful once its scope and learning purpose are clear. Money is not a requirement for successful discovery; facts and disconfirmation are useful outcomes. A promised follow-up is not completed purchase evidence.

## T9–T11. Reusable notes and debrief

Create one card per observation:

> Evidence ID: [ ]  
> Participant / segment / date / source location: [ ]  
> Exact quote if captured verbatim, otherwise labeled paraphrase: [ ]  
> Type: observed behavior / reported past event / claim / opinion / hypothetical / request / commitment  
> Signal: problem / goal / workaround / obstacle / budget / buying criterion / follow-up  
> Context and question: [ ]  
> Interpretation: [ ]  
> Unknown / contradiction / reliability limit: [ ]

Immediately afterward, review with the founder and product/business perspectives: What changed for each goal? Which evidence contradicted the idea? What remains unsupported? Which stakeholder is missing? Update the next three questions and put an owner and date against the next learning action. Store raw notes alongside searchable cards, separately from product ideas.

Five-minute version: establish their current priority, reconstruct one event, ask what they did and why it mattered, close, then record evidence.
